Homeschooling: The Ultimate "Choose Your Own Adventure"

There isn't one right way to homeschool — there are nearly endless paths. Here's a friendly map of the most popular methods to help you find (or blend) yours.

ME
Mindi Eldredge · July 25, 2026 · 8 min read

Do you remember the Choose Your Own Adventure books that were so popular in the 1980s and 1990s? They were the stories where, every few pages, you reached a decision point. Would you explore the mysterious cave? Turn to page 42. Climb the mountain instead? Turn to page 67. Every choice led to a completely different adventure.

I've often thought that homeschooling is the ultimate Choose Your Own Adventure story. The difference is that instead of choosing between two or three paths, homeschooling offers nearly endless possibilities. There isn't just one "right" way to educate your children. You have the freedom to create an educational journey that fits your family's values, your children's personalities, and your goals.

That can feel exciting… and maybe even a little overwhelming! With so many options available, where do you begin?

Let's take a look at some of the most popular homeschooling methods. These descriptions are intentionally brief, but in future posts I'll dive deeper into each one to help you determine which approach—or combination of approaches—might be the best fit for your family.

(The following are listed in no particular order.)

Charlotte Mason Method

The Charlotte Mason method is built on the belief that children are whole people who deserve a rich, meaningful education. Instead of relying on dry textbooks, students learn through living books, time spent outdoors, nature study, art, music, poetry, narration, and short lessons that encourage focused attention. The goal is not simply to fill children with information but to cultivate wisdom, character, curiosity, and a lifelong love of learning.

Classical Education Method

Classical education draws inspiration from the traditional liberal arts and follows three developmental stages known as the Trivium: Grammar, Logic, and Rhetoric. Younger children build a strong foundation of knowledge, middle-grade students learn to think critically and analyze ideas, and older students develop the ability to communicate persuasively. This method emphasizes great literature, history, languages, and thoughtful discussion.

Waldorf Education Method

Waldorf education focuses on educating the whole child—head, heart, and hands. Lessons emphasize creativity, imagination, storytelling, handcrafts, music, movement, and time in nature. Formal academics are introduced more gradually than in many other approaches, with an emphasis on nurturing children's emotional, artistic, and physical development alongside intellectual growth.

Heart Education Method

Heart education emphasizes developing the whole person by nurturing both character and intellect. Relationships, meaningful conversations, inspiring stories, service, and personal growth are woven throughout learning. While academics remain important, equal emphasis is placed on cultivating virtues, strengthening family connections, and helping children develop a lifelong desire to seek truth, goodness, and beauty.

Hybrid Homeschooling

Hybrid homeschooling combines learning at home with classes taught outside the home. Children may attend a homeschool co-op, university model school, microschool, enrichment program, or private school for part of the week while completing the remainder of their education at home. This approach provides flexibility while allowing students to benefit from classroom experiences and specialized instruction.

Montessori Method

The Montessori method encourages children to become independent, self-motivated learners. Carefully prepared learning environments allow students to choose activities that match their developmental readiness while parents or teachers act as guides rather than lecturers. Hands-on materials, practical life skills, and self-directed exploration play a central role in this educational philosophy.

Thomas Jefferson Education Method

Also known as TJEd or Leadership Education, the Thomas Jefferson Education method emphasizes inspiring a love of learning before focusing on rigorous academics. It encourages students to read classic works, discuss great ideas, and gradually assume responsibility for their own education. Parents serve primarily as mentors who model lifelong learning and help prepare children to become capable leaders and contributors to society.

Unit Studies Method

Rather than studying each subject separately, the Unit Studies method integrates multiple subjects around one central topic. For example, while learning about ancient Egypt, children might read historical accounts, write reports, explore geography, study science related to the Nile River, create artwork, and solve math problems connected to the civilization. This integrated approach helps children make meaningful connections across subjects.

School-at-Home Method

The School-at-Home method closely resembles a traditional classroom experience. Families often use complete curriculum packages with textbooks, workbooks, lesson plans, quizzes, and tests organized by grade level. This approach provides structure and familiarity and is often appealing to families transitioning from public or private school.

Unschooling Method

Unschooling is a child-led educational philosophy built around the belief that children learn best by pursuing their own interests. Rather than following a predetermined curriculum, parents provide rich opportunities, resources, and experiences while allowing children to explore topics that naturally capture their curiosity. Learning becomes woven into everyday life through conversations, projects, travel, reading, and real-world experiences.

Eclectic Method

The Eclectic method recognizes that no single homeschooling philosophy fits every family or every child. Instead, parents intentionally combine ideas, resources, and practices from multiple educational methods to create a customized learning experience. One family might blend Charlotte Mason's living books with Montessori activities, Classical memory work, Unit Studies, and hands-on science. This flexibility makes eclectic homeschooling one of the most common approaches among experienced homeschool families.

So… Which Adventure Will You Choose?

One of the greatest blessings of homeschooling is that you're not locked into a single path. You might begin with one philosophy and gradually discover that another fits your family even better. You may even borrow your favorite ideas from several methods and create an approach that's uniquely your own.

That's the beauty of homeschooling. Every family's story is different, and every educational adventure is one of a kind.
